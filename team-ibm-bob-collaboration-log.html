A complete record of everything the team and IBM Bob worked through together — from the first idea to the final shipped prototype. Covers problem framing, design decisions, what was prioritized, what was built, and how Bob was embedded into the product itself.

1 Where We Started — The Problem We Agreed On

The team came to Bob with the 2026 Ivy Tech IT Challenge brief and a focused concern: first-semester students placed in co-requisite math (MATH 136 College Algebra + MATH 036 Algebra Support) don't know what to study or when to ask for help — not until after they've already failed their first exam. By the time the signal arrives, it's too late.

The core insight the team brought: The failure isn't a knowledge gap alone — it's a visibility gap. Students can't see their own skill holes, can't connect them to deadlines, and can't navigate the support resources that exist. Fix the visibility, and the help-seeking behaviour follows.

Bob helped the team sharpen that framing into a single, testable user story the whole prototype could be anchored to:

Starter User Story (agreed with Bob):
"As a first-semester student in co-requisite math, I can get a weekly study plan with tutoring options so that I pass my first college math course."

3 The Two Roles IBM Bob Played

One of the cleaner decisions in the project was recognising that Bob had two completely different jobs — and keeping them distinct.

Bob
Role 1
Planning partner — before any code was written

Bob was used to reason through the user story, design the context-injection approach, work out the resource-matching logic, and produce the full sub-task build plan. The team could ask "what should the prompt contain?" or "how do we handle schedule overlap?" and get structured, concrete answers that directly shaped the architecture.

This is where the structured context block format was designed — the [STUDENT CONTEXT] header, the score lines with ⚠ flags, the resource list sorted by topic overlap, and the [END CONTEXT] footer. All of that was worked out in conversation with Bob before app.js existed.

Bob
Role 2
Live AI coach — embedded inside the running product

In the deployed prototype, Bob is the AI engine powering the chat panel. Every time a student sends a message, app.js builds a fresh context block from window.CASE_DATA and prepends it to the student's question before sending to Bob. Bob replies with a personalised weekly study plan and tutoring recommendations.

Bob does not make autonomous decisions. It surfaces advice grounded in the provided dataset and always points the student toward a human tutor, instructor, or coach for high-stakes questions. The team was deliberate about this boundary.

4 The Build — Every Sub-Task, What Changed, What Was Decided

Sub-Task 1
Student Selector Screen Done

Needed: an entry point that shows all five students and gates the dashboard until one is selected. The team decided this had to be the first thing a demo judge sees — it establishes the multi-student nature of the prototype immediately.

  • Created index.html with two top-level sections: #selector-screen and #dashboard-screen (hidden by default).
  • app.js loops over window.CASE_DATA.students and renders one button per student showing name, program, modality, and work hours.
  • On click, the selected student_id is stored and the selector hides; the dashboard appears.
  • A "↩ Switch student" button in the header allows resetting without a page reload.
Sub-Task 2
Student Profile Card Done

Decision: show identity and context at the top of every dashboard view so the student (and the judge) always know whose data they're looking at without scrolling.

  • Renders: first name, program, modality, work hours/week, class days, class time, location, section.
  • Data pulled from students, enrollments, and course_schedule tables — joined in renderProfile(id).
  • Styled with a distinct highlight colour to visually anchor the dashboard.
Sub-Task 3
Diagnostic Scores Card Done

This card is the data foundation for everything else. The team and Bob agreed that the threshold for "weak" should be < 70% — consistent with common academic pass standards and simple enough to explain to a student.

  • Each topic row shows a colour-coded bar: green ≥ 70%, amber 50–69%, red < 50%.
  • Weak topics are labelled "Review" (amber) or "Needs work" (red).
  • These same weak topics drive resource matching and the Bob context block.
  • An empty-state message handles students with no diagnostic data yet — this was flagged as a real risk and handled explicitly.
Sub-Task 4
Upcoming Assignments Card Done

Decision: show a 14-day window, not the whole semester. The goal is urgency and relevance — the student needs to know what's happening this week and next, not in April.

  • Filters assignment_calendar to the student's enrolled courses (MATH 136 + MATH 036) and the next 14 days from TODAY.
  • TODAY is set to the actual current date (new Date()), so the demo is always live.
  • Items sorted by due date ascending; type badges (Exam, Quiz, Homework) visually distinguish urgency.
  • Deadlines are also injected into the Bob context block so Bob can reference them in study plan advice.
Sub-Task 5
Support Resources Card Done

This was the most discussed sub-task. The team wanted resources that actually fit the student's situation — not a generic list of everything Ivy Tech offers. Bob helped design the two-filter approach during planning.

  • Filter 1 (topic relevance): resources whose topics field overlaps with the student's weak topics, or equals "All".
  • Filter 2 (schedule fit): cross-reference the resource's day/time against the student's study_availability windows. Output: "✓ Fits your schedule", "Online — anytime", or "Outside your availability".
  • Online resources always pass the schedule filter — a deliberate choice to ensure no student sees an empty list.
  • Matched resources are also listed in the Bob context block so Bob can name them specifically in recommendations.
Sub-Task 6
IBM Bob Chat Panel Done Core AI requirement

The centrepiece of the prototype. The team and Bob designed the context-injection pattern here — the key architectural decision of the whole project.

  • Context block (buildContext(id)): assembled fresh on every message. Contains student name, program, modality, work hours, all topic scores (with ⚠ on weak ones), upcoming deadlines, study availability, and matched support resources.
  • Opening auto-message: on student selection, the app automatically sends a first message as the student — "Hi! I'm [name]… Can you give me a study plan for this week?" — so the judge immediately sees a personalised response without typing anything.
  • Receive handler: Bob's replies are captured via the receive event on the watsonx Assistant instance and rendered as chat bubbles in the custom panel.
  • Built-in launcher hidden: the Watson Assistant floating launcher is suppressed (--WatsonAssistantChat-launcher-Display: none) so only the team's custom chat UI is visible.
  • Credentials placeholder: integrationID, serviceInstanceID, and region are left as labelled placeholders in app.js for the team to fill in before the live demo.
Sub-Task 7
README + 2030 Impact Statement Done

Both written deliverables were drafted with Bob and grounded directly in the case brief, the dataset, and research on early-alert interventions.

  • README.md: five required sections — The Problem, The Solution, The Technical Approach, The Pillar and Statewide Strategy, How IBM Bob Was Used.
  • IMPACT_STATEMENT.md: who benefits (students, instructors, advisors, institution), metric and estimated movement (5–10 percentage points on MATH 136 pass rate, ~2,000 additional students passing per year at Ivy Tech scale), 7-step rollout roadmap to 2030, and the one risk to watch.

5 Key Design Decisions — Logged

Context injection over system prompts

Rather than relying on a one-time system prompt, the team chose to rebuild the student context block on every single message. This means Bob always has current, complete data even if the conversation has been going for a while. The tradeoff is a slightly larger message payload — accepted as worthwhile at prototype scale.

Rule-based matching over ML

The dataset has five students, a fixed list of topics, and a small resource catalogue. Using embeddings or ranking models would have been technically impressive and practically wrong for this problem. Rules are transparent — a student or instructor can read the matching logic and understand exactly why a resource was recommended.

The 70% threshold

The team chose 70% as the weak-topic cutoff. This is the standard academic pass threshold and is immediately legible to students and instructors. It also drives both the visual UI (colour coding) and the AI inputs (what Bob is told to focus on).

No server, no login

The prototype runs entirely from a single index.html. This was a deliberate simplicity choice: a demo judge can download the repo and open one file. No environment setup, no npm install, no cloud credentials to provision. The only configuration needed is the IBM Bob embed credentials in app.js.

Bob's advisory boundary

Both in the product and in the written deliverables, the team was explicit: Bob surfaces recommendations, it does not make decisions. High-stakes questions (e.g. "Should I drop this course?") always include a prompt to speak with an instructor or advisor. The Impact Statement named this the "AI supports, a person decides" principle.

7 The Risk the Team Named

The Impact Statement was explicit about the one risk the team considered most serious:

Risk: Students follow the coach's recommendations but still fail because the recommendations are only as good as the diagnostic data. If a student's diagnostic scores are incomplete, outdated, or missing — common in the first two weeks of a semester — the coach may recommend the wrong resources or give false reassurance.

Mitigation: Always surface a "talk to your instructor" fallback. Flag students with no diagnostic data as "data not yet available" rather than showing empty recommendations. Never let the coach tell a student they're on track without at least one human check-in.

This mitigation is built into the prototype today — the scores card handles missing data with an explicit empty-state message rather than silently skipping the card.
